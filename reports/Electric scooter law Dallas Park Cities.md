# Mapping Where Your Private E-Scooter Rides Legally

Texas law lets a conforming stand-up e-scooter be ridden with **no driver's license, registration, insurance, inspection or state helmet requirement**. It may use streets posted **35 mph or less**, sidewalks, and paths reserved exclusively for bicycles. The same statute also lets any city take those places away on a safety determination, and all three of your cities have done so to different degrees.

**Dallas leaves the most room.** You can ride on public streets posted 30 mph or less (the codified citywide default) and in bike lanes on those streets, at 20 mph or less, with a white headlamp and a red rear reflector or lamp at night. You cannot ride on sidewalks, trails, parks or plazas. Arterials posted exactly 35 mph are legal under state law, but they are a gray zone in Dallas: the City publishes a "35 MPH or higher" no-ride rule, and the code text behind it could not be read.

**University Park** permits riders **18 and older** only on a handful of SMU-border segments: Airline Rd., SMU Blvd., Bush Ave., and listed Hillcrest and Daniel sidewalks. SMU's private campus is also open under SMU's own rules. Riding anywhere else in University Park is a citable offense; SMU says the fine is $136.

**Highland Park** bans operating any motor-assisted scooter on any public right-of-way or any outdoor area open to the public, including privately owned ones. The only lawful riding there is on private property closed to the public.

**DART rail** lets you carry a scooter aboard in the wheelchair and stroller area. That is the cleanest lawful way to bridge the zones where riding is banned.

**If you are challenged, your best protection is facts and text:** proof of the scooter's specifications, the posted limit, the exact location, your age, and the ordinance version in force. Preemption is a weak shield. No court has held any of these local bans invalid, and § 551.352(b) expressly authorizes bans on streets and sidewalks.

## 1. State law permits the ride, and cities may switch it off

### 1.1 Texas classifies the stand-up scooter as a "motor-assisted scooter"

Everything favorable to you turns on the device meeting the definition of **"motor-assisted scooter"** in Transportation Code **§ 551.351**. A scooter that meets it gets two things:
- **§ 551.352(e)** removes every Title 7 provision "applicable to a motor vehicle".
- **§ 551.352(d)** imports the bicycle rules.

The definition has **no speed cap and no wattage cap**. Its only motor limit, "not exceeding 40 cubic centimeters," measures piston displacement, which an electric hub motor does not have. No AG opinion, appellate case, or DPS/TxDOT/TxDMV guidance applying the 40-cc limit to electric motors was found ([FindLaw § 551.351](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/transportation-code/transp-sect-551-351/); [TTI officer guide](https://cades.tti.tamu.edu/files/2024/06/LE_mircomobility_v2.pdf)).

A seatless, kickable stand-up scooter is therefore a motor-assisted scooter on the face of the text. A 1,000 W-plus, 30–40 mph machine invites a purposive argument that it is not the "low-powered" device the chapter title contemplates. The Legislature considered replacing the cc limit with a **2,000-watt electric cap** in S.B. 549 (2019), but did not enact it ([S.B. 549 committee substitute](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/html/SB00549S.htm)).

**If the scooter is not a motor-assisted scooter,** nothing better replaces that status. It falls to the generic "self-propelled vehicle" class, so it is a motor vehicle. It cannot be titled without a manufacturer's certificate of origin and FMVSS compliance. That exposes the rider to charges of riding with no registration, no license and no insurance ([TxDMV, Register Your Vehicle](https://www.txdmv.gov/motorists/register-your-vehicle); [TxDMV, Assembled Vehicles](https://www.txdmv.gov/motorists/assembled-vehicles)).

The **DWI** statutes sit outside this framework. Penal Code § 49.01 borrows the § 32.34(a) definition of "motor vehicle": "a device in, on, or by which a person or property is or may be transported or drawn on a highway". Section 551.352(e) reaches only Transportation Code Title 7, so a scooter rider on a street is probably exposed to DWI. One researcher extract-verified this definition ([FindLaw Penal § 32.34](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/penal-code/penal-sect-32-34/)); another could not confirm its current wording. No Texas appellate case deciding scooter DWI was found. Rating: **subject to interpretation, high risk**.

## 2. Every lawful way to ride, surface by surface

### 2.1 Streets: Dallas below 35 mph, University Park only around SMU, Highland Park never

State law is the ceiling. A conforming scooter may use any street or highway posted **35 mph or less** and may cross faster roads **only at an intersection** ([FindLaw § 551.352](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/transportation-code/transp-sect-551-352/)).

**In Dallas**, the 2018 amendment was adopted to "allow use of motor assisted scooters in public rights-of-way" ([June 2018 council briefing](https://dallascityhall.com/government/Council%20Meeting%20Documents/motor-assisted-scooter-ordinance-discussion_combined_060618.pdf)). 2026 reporting says riders "must instead use bike lanes or ride in the street with traffic" ([Dallas Morning News, 2026](https://www.dallasnews.com/news/public-safety/article/dallas-arlington-scooter-rules-world-cup-22200189.php)). Dallas's codified default limit is **30 mph** on any street not otherwise listed (Sec. 28-43; [amlegal](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/dallas/latest/dallas_tx/0-0-0-112648)). Every unlisted residential street therefore sits below both the state's 35-mph ceiling and Dallas's "35 or higher" no-ride threshold.

On those streets you ride under the bicycle duties:
- keep to the right curb (§ 551.103);
- have a working brake, and a front lamp and rear reflector at night (§ 551.104);
- carry no passengers.

The City also states a **20 mph cap**.

Streets posted **exactly 35 mph** are legal under state law but fall inside the City's published "35 MPH or higher" no-ride wording. Rating: **ambiguous** until the § 28-41.1.1 text is read. Streets posted **above 35** are prohibited except for crossing at an intersection.

**In University Park**, public streets and alleys are closed to scooters by § 12.01.011(a). There is one exception: riders **18 or older** may ride the street on **Airline Road (SMU Boulevard to Daniel), SMU Boulevard and Bush Avenue** ([eCode360](https://ecode360.com/40048914)). Hillcrest and Daniel segments are sidewalk-only, so riding in the street there falls outside the exception. A 2018 KRLD report gives the logic: where SMU owns both sides of a street, the street and sidewalk are allowed; where SMU owns one side, only the sidewalk is ([KRLD](https://krld.radio.com/articles/university-park-allow-use-electric-scooters)).

**In Highland Park**, no street riding is lawful. Every street, alley and parkway is "public right-of-way" under § 12.09.002 ([eCode360](https://ecode360.com/39247224)).

### 2.2 Sidewalks: the state allows them, and all three cities withdraw the permission

State law affirmatively permits sidewalks under § 551.352(d), and the motor-vehicle sidewalk ban in § 545.422 does not reach a conforming scooter. But § 551.352(b) lets cities prohibit sidewalk riding, and all three have:
- **Dallas** bans sidewalk riding citywide, for private and rented scooters alike, under the City's stated reading of § 28-41.1.1 ([Community Impact](https://communityimpact.com/dallas-fort-worth/lake-highlands-lakewood/transportation/2023/04/12/dallas-implements-new-rules-for-electric-scooter-riders-providers-in-program-relaunch/)).
- **Highland Park** bans every sidewalk.
- **University Park** bans every sidewalk except the listed ones, for riders 18 and older:
  - both sides of Airline (SMU Blvd. to Daniel), SMU Blvd. and Bush Ave.;
  - the east sidewalk of Hillcrest south of Daniel;
  - the south sidewalk of Daniel from Hillcrest to Airline;
  - both Daniel sidewalks from Airline to Durham.

  The Hillcrest and Daniel items are paraphrase only.

**No other sidewalk exception exists in any of the three codes as far as the extracts show.** The Dallas "start or end of ride" exception is secondary-source only and should not be relied on ([BikeDFW](https://www.bikedfw.org/bike--scooter-share.html)).

### 2.3 Bike lanes and shared-use paths: on-street lanes follow the street's speed limit

**On-street bike lanes.** Section 551.352(a) is written in terms of "a street or highway", and a striped bike lane is part of that roadway. On this reasoning (an inference, not a holding), a bike lane takes the posted limit of its street. A lane on a 30-mph Dallas street is usable. A lane on a 40-mph arterial is not, except for crossing at an intersection. Dallas guidance points riders to bike lanes, and no ordinance requiring their use was found ([DMN 2026](https://www.dallasnews.com/news/public-safety/article/dallas-arlington-scooter-rules-world-cup-22200189.php)). The City's [Existing Bicycle Infrastructure map](https://dallascityhall.com/departments/transportation/bikeway/Pages/default.aspx) is the authoritative list. No bike lanes or sharrows were found in Highland Park or University Park. Rating: **subject to interpretation** for lanes on roads posted above 35.

**Physically separated paths "set aside for the exclusive operation of bicycles"** are expressly allowed by § 551.352(d). Two complications:
- Dallas's 2018 Ord. 31048 banned scooters on exactly those paths ([Ord. 31048](https://files.amlegal.com/pdffiles/Dallas/31048.pdf)). That is the most preemption-vulnerable provision in this report (Section 7).
- Few if any Dallas facilities are exclusively for bicycles. The Harwood Street separated "micro-mobility" lanes were a pilot whose permanence is unconfirmed ([Better Block](https://www.betterblock.org/bike-harwood)). Whether a protected lane counts as a "path" or as part of the street is **subject to interpretation**.

**Shared-use hike-and-bike trails** are, by § 545.422(c), designed for "pedestrians, bicyclists, or both." They are not "exclusive" to bicycles, so § 551.352(d) grants no state-law right there, and access turns on the owner's rules (**ambiguous** at state level). Dallas closes them to scooters: "Motorized scooters are not permitted in City of Dallas parks or on trails" ([Dallas Parks](https://www.dallasparks.org/150/Etiquette-Emergency-System)). The bans named in sources are:
- the Katy Trail ("No motorized vehicles permitted," [Friends of the Katy Trail](https://katytraildallas.org/trail-info/));
- White Rock Lake;
- more than 30 Lake Highlands and Lakewood parks ([Community Impact](https://communityimpact.com/dallas-fort-worth/lake-highlands-lakewood/transportation/2023/04/12/dallas-implements-new-rules-for-electric-scooter-riders-providers-in-program-relaunch/)).

The Santa Fe, SoPac, Northaven and Trinity trails are not named in any source, but they are City park facilities and lean covered. The **Ronald Kirk Pedestrian Bridge** carve-out ("may not operate on any public trail, except for the Ronald Kirk Pedestrian Bridge") is a rental Program Rule ([City memo, Apr. 10, 2026](https://dallascityhall.com/government/citymanager/Documents/FY25-26%20Memos/Combined%20Memos_04102026.pdf)). It is **uncertain** for private riders.

Highland Park's right-of-way definition expressly includes a "public transportation path", so its paths are banned. University Park park paths are covered by § 1.09.032 (single extract), and any sidewalk-like path by § 12.01.011.

### 2.5 Alternative vehicle classifications: no modification legally upgrades a stand-up scooter

For your device, the motor-assisted scooter classification is both **the one available and the most favorable**. Every other class is either unreachable or worse.

Real, functional changes can change classification:
- a true pedal drivetrain;
- a motor change;
- a seat or saddle;
- a speed cap the user cannot defeat.

Cosmetic changes do not. No Texas case or AG opinion squarely addresses reclassification of a scooter by modification. **Disguising a device is never a lawful option.** That includes "e-bike" decals, fake § 664.002 labels, dummy cranks, and anything that misstates speed or power. Section 664.002 requires the e-bike label to stay accurate after any modification ([Ch. 664](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/TN/htm/TN.664.htm)).

## 5. Police stops: what the officer may do and what you must say

**Reasonable suspicion is a low bar; conviction is not.** An officer may briefly detain you on reasonable suspicion of any offense, including a fine-only Class C. Under *Heien v. North Carolina*, 574 U.S. 54 (2014), a stop can rest on an **objectively reasonable mistake of law** ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/574/54/); quotations unverified, from recollection). The Court said "an officer can gain no Fourth Amendment advantage through a sloppy study of the laws he is duty-bound to enforce". Justice Kagan's concurrence limits reasonable mistakes to genuinely ambiguous statutes requiring "hard interpretive work". Under *Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979), an officer may enforce a duly enacted ordinance even if a court later voids it ([Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/443/31/); recollection).

Applied here:
- A Dallas officer who stops you on a road posted exactly 35 mph, relying on the City's "35 MPH or higher" wording, probably made a reasonable stop. That is true even if the code actually says "greater than 35".
- The **conviction** still requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the law in force prohibited your conduct (Penal Code § 2.01; recollection).
- An officer in a place with no local sidewalk ban who stops you for "sidewalk riding" makes an unreasonable mistake, because § 551.352(d) plainly permits it. Suppression under CCP art. 38.23 would then help only with evidence found after the stop, not with the riding the officer observed.

How Texas's Court of Criminal Appeals treats *Heien* was **not verified**.

**Citation versus arrest.** Under *Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001), and CCP art. 14.01(b), an officer **may** arrest for a fine-only offense committed in view. Citation in lieu of arrest under art. 14.06(b) is discretionary. Transportation Code § 543.004 makes a written notice to appear mandatory only for speeding and open-container offenses (all recollection; [CCP ch. 14](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.14.htm); [Transp. Code ch. 543](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/TN/htm/TN.543.htm)). Public intoxication is excluded from citation, so an intoxicated rider should expect custody.

**Identification under Penal Code § 38.02.** While merely detained, you commit no offense by declining to give your name, address and date of birth. Giving **false** information while detained is an offense (Class B). After a **lawful arrest**, refusing those three items is a Class C offense (recollection; [Penal Code ch. 38](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.38.htm)). In practice, refusing ID at a Class C stop usually converts a citation into an arrest, because a citation needs an identified person to sign a promise to appear. That is an inference.

**No driver's license is required.** Chapter 521 governs operating a "motor vehicle", and § 551.352(e) removes those provisions from scooters. The failed S.B. 549 would have *added* a license requirement, which confirms none exists ([Plural Policy S.B. 549](https://open.pluralpolicy.com/tx/bills/86/SB549)). Highland Park's and University Park's driver's-license rules apply to **e-bikes**, not scooters.

**Facts the officer needs to classify the device.** Be ready to show, with a manufacturer spec sheet or photos:
- the number of wheels;
- that there is **no seat** and no pedals;
- the standing deck;
- that it can be **kicked along**;
- the working brake;
- that the motor is electric, and its wattage;
- its top speed;
- its lights;
- that it has no VIN.

Together these satisfy § 551.351 and rule out the e-bike, moped and pocket-bike classes.

**Impound.** No Texas statute specifically authorizes impounding a privately owned scooter for a Class C violation. The abandoned-vehicle law (ch. 683) is built for motor vehicles, and Chapter 59 forfeiture does not reach Class C offenses. Inventory or safekeeping is constitutional mainly when you are **arrested** and no reasonable alternative exists, under a standardized policy (*South Dakota v. Opperman*; *Colorado v. Bertine*; all recollection; [Justia Opperman](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/428/364/)). For a cited, compliant rider, there is no evident basis to keep the scooter; you walk it away or hand it to a companion. That is subject to interpretation.

**Recording.** The Fifth Circuit recognizes a First Amendment right to film police performing duties in public, subject to reasonable time, place and manner limits (*Turner v. Driver*, 848 F.3d 678 (5th Cir. 2017); recollection). Penal Code § 38.15(d) provides a "speech only" defense to interference, but physical obstruction is not protected. Texas is a one-party-consent state (§ 16.02). A 2023 25-foot "buffer" bill did not pass, and **2025 legislation was not verified** (all recollection; [Penal Code ch. 38](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.38.htm)).

**Preserving evidence.** At the scene and within hours, record the following:
- photos or video of the nearest **speed-limit sign** and any no-ride or trail sign;
- the exact location, using a GPS pin and the cross-street;
- whether you were in the street, a bike lane, a crosswalk at an intersection, or on a sidewalk;
- the time, and the lighting conditions;
- your scooter's lights working;
- the scooter itself (deck, no seat);
- the officer's name and badge number, the agency, and whether a body camera was visible.

Write a dated narrative the same day.

**DWI.** Do not treat the motor-vehicle exemption as protection from DWI. The Penal Code uses its own definition (Section 1.1).

### 6.2 Gather evidence through discovery, public records and the ordinance history

**Discovery.** Since **January 1, 2025**, municipal-court procedure has been governed by **CCP Chapter 45A**, which replaced Chapter 45 ([CCP ch. 45A](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.45A.htm)). Art. 45A.003 imports the Code's general provisions where 45A is silent ([Justia Subch. A](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/code-of-criminal-procedure/title-1/chapter-45a/subchapter-a/)). That brings in **art. 39.14 discovery**, chapter 24 subpoenas, art. 28.01 pretrial hearings, and art. 27.08/27.09 exceptions.

Serve a written art. 39.14 request on the **city prosecutor** for:
- the offense report and the officer's notes;
- body-worn and dash video;
- photos;
- any speed-measurement data.

Under art. 39.14(h) the State must disclose exculpatory or impeaching information. A pro se defendant may be limited to inspection rather than copies under art. 39.14(d). Both points are recollection; check [CCP ch. 39](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.39.htm).

**Public Information Act.** You can also send a PIA request to the police department, but it is slower. The law-enforcement exception (§ 552.108) often blocks release while a case is pending, though "basic information" stays public. No authority was found that a PIA request tolls any court deadline (recollection; [Gov't Code ch. 552](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/GV/htm/GV.552.htm)).

**Body-camera footage.** Occupations Code § 1701.661 requires a written request stating:
- the date and approximate time of the recording;
- the specific location;
- the name of a person who is the subject of the recording.

Recordings of fine-only misdemeanor stops that did not end in arrest can be released only **with the subject's written authorization**, so **sign and include one** (recollection, moderate-high confidence; [Occ. Code ch. 1701](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/OC/htm/OC.1701.htm)). The assignment's framing of § 1701.660 conflicts with the researcher's recollection that it governs "Recordings as Evidence" in deadly-force and misconduct matters. Verify before citing it.

**Ordinance version in effect.** Read the section's history note. Then obtain **certified copies** of each amending ordinance in force on the offense date from the Dallas City Secretary, the Highland Park Town Secretary, or the University Park City Secretary. Ask the court to take judicial notice under Tex. R. Evid. 204; certified copies are self-authenticating under Rule 902(4) (both recollection). Pull the adoption record as well (agenda, minutes, preamble):
- Dallas Ord. 31048 and the 2022 amendment;
- Highland Park Ord. 2042 and Ord. 2150;
- University Park Ord. 18-048/18-049 and the 2009 ordinance.

That record shows whether a § 551.352(b) safety determination was made.

### 6.4 Contesting: pleas, trial, deferral and appeal

**Pleas.** You may plead **not guilty**, **nolo contendere**, or **guilty**. A nolo plea cannot be used as an admission in a civil suit (art. 27.02(5); recollection). If you plead not guilty, the State must file a conforming complaint unless you agree in writing to proceed on the citation (art. 27.14(d); recollection).

**Trial.** You may request a **jury trial**. A municipal jury has six members (former art. 45.027, per [TMCEC](https://www.tmcec.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/08-Level-I-Ch-5.pdf)). Alternatively, waive the jury in writing under art. 45A.155 ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/2024/code-of-criminal-procedure/title-1/chapter-45a/subchapter-d/article-45a-155/)). There is no right to appointed counsel for a fine-only offense (art. 1.051; recollection).

**Deferred disposition** is the main conviction-avoiding outcome. Under art. 45A.302, on a plea or finding of guilt in a fine-only case, the judge may defer for up to **180 days** without adjudicating guilt. The judge may impose a "special expense fee" no greater than the maximum fine, plus court costs ([Justia 45A.302](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/code-of-criminal-procedure/title-1/chapter-45a/subchapter-g/article-45a-302/)). Conditions may include "another course as directed by the judge" (art. 45A.303).

**Driving-safety-course dismissal is likely unavailable.** It requires an offense that "involves the operation of a motor vehicle" (former art. 45.0511, presumed carried into 45A.351 et seq.; [TMCEC Bench Book](https://www.tmcec.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Chapter-5-Driving-Safety-Courses.pdf)). A scooter is not a motor vehicle under Title 7.

**Inability to pay.** Arts. 45A.252, .254 and .257 provide for an ability-to-pay inquiry, community service credited at no less than $100 per 8 hours, and waiver for indigence ([TMCEC blog](https://tmcecblog.com/2025/01/27/protections-regarding-fines-and-fees-in-chapter-45a-safeguarding-justice-for-all/)).

**Appeal from Dallas (court of record).** There is **no trial de novo**. The appeal is decided on the record by the Dallas County criminal courts with appellate jurisdiction (Gov't Code § 30.00811; [SPA *Villa* summary](https://www.spa.texas.gov/past-pdr-cases/villa-whitney-s)). To preserve it:
- **ask for a court reporter or recording at trial**;
- file a written motion for new trial **within 10 days** setting out the points of error;
- post a bond of the greater of $100 or double the fine and costs.

The bond and deadline figures come from a city guide and a 1999 bill analysis, not statute text ([North Richland Hills guide](https://www.nrhtx.com/DocumentCenter/View/620); [HBA H.B. 651](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/76R/analysis/html/HB00651I.htm)). The ch. 30 section numbers are recollection.

**Appeal from Highland Park or University Park (likely non-record; flag the uncertainty).** The steps are:
- file a motion for new trial within **5 days** of judgment (art. 45A.201);
- file the **appeal bond within 10 days after judgment is entered** (art. 45A.203). Do not wait for a ruling on the motion. A late bond leaves the county court without jurisdiction.

The minimum bond is at least $50. The former statute set it at two times the fine and costs.

The appeal is a **trial de novo** in county court (art. 45A.202: "a de novo trial shall be held on appeal unless the appeal is taken from a municipal court of record"; [texas.public.law](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._code_of_crim._proc._article_45a.202)). Highland Park's non-record status rests on dated secondary sources ([Avvo](https://www.avvo.com/legal-answers/what-is-the-process-for-appealing-a-highland-park--2492460.html)). **Confirm with each clerk before trial.**

**Further appeal.** Appeal beyond county court is barred where the fine is $100 or less, unless the sole issue is the constitutionality of the statute or ordinance (art. 4.03; recollection).

## 7. Are the local bans vulnerable? Arguably in places, never held invalid

**No court has held any Dallas, Highland Park or University Park scooter rule invalid.** No Texas appellate decision or AG opinion construing § 551.352(b) was found. Everything below is "arguably invalid", not "held invalid".

The framework has four parts:
- All three cities are **home-rule** and need only freedom from a clear state limit ([LGC § 51.072](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._local_gov't_code_title_2_subtitle_d_chapter_51)).
- Courts harmonize a statute and an ordinance where any reasonable construction allows ([*Santa Fe v. Young*](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-court-of-appeals/1013171.html), quoting *Dallas Merchant's*).
- The **express** uniformity clause in § 542.201 bars conflicting local traffic rules "unless expressly authorized". That shifts the question from legislative intent to whether a particular rule **conflicts with § 551.352** and falls outside **§ 551.352(b)**.
- Where a statute expressly limits cities, the Texas Supreme Court reads it strictly (*Laredo*, [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2018/16-0748.html)).

**H.B. 2127 is irrelevant.** It reaches eight codes, and the Transportation Code is not one of them (recollection).

## 8. Recommendations ranked by certainty, cost and ticket risk

### 8.1 Best legal way to ride in each jurisdiction

**Dallas.** Ride in the street or in a bike lane on streets posted **30 mph or less**:
- at **no more than 20 mph**;
- as near the right curb as practicable, except where § 551.103 exceptions apply;
- with a working brake;
- at night, with a white front lamp visible at 500 feet and a red rear reflector or lamp;
- carrying no passenger.

Cross roads posted above 35 **only at intersections**; walking across 35-mph roads removes any Dallas doubt. **Never ride sidewalks, trails (including the Katy Trail and White Rock Lake), parks or plazas.** The 9 p.m. curfew and the 10 mph slow-ride zones appear to be rental rules that do not bind you. The curfew is rated ambiguous, leaning rental-only, and no source applies the slow zones to private riders. Following the slow-zone speeds anyway costs little.

**Highland Park.** **No lawful public riding exists.** Ride only on private property closed to the public, with the owner's consent. Carry or walk the scooter through Town; a dismounted rider counts as a pedestrian. Do not park it on Town right-of-way. Use Dallas streets or DART rail to route around the Town.

**University Park.** If you are **18 or older**, ride only:
- on the street or sidewalks of Airline Rd. (SMU Blvd. to Daniel), SMU Blvd. and Bush Ave.;
- on the listed Hillcrest and Daniel **sidewalks**, staying on the sidewalk;
- on SMU's interior campus under Policy 9.14.

Keep to **10 mph**. Everything else in University Park is off-limits, including Snider Plaza and the parks.

### 8.4 Scooter specifications to confirm

Confirm each of the following, ideally from the manufacturer's spec sheet:
- **No seat or saddle.** This keeps the scooter out of the moped, motorcycle and pocket-bike classes.
- **Motor rated and peak wattage.** This is the 40-cc ambiguity; the risk rises above about 1,000 W.
- **Top speed and any non-defeatable firmware cap.** NHTSA's informal line is about 20 mph.
- **Wheel count and size.**
- **Kickability.** The scooter must be able to be propelled by human power alone.
- **Brakes** able to skid a wheel on dry pavement.
- **A white front lamp visible at 500 ft, and a red rear reflector or a red lamp visible at 500 ft.**
- **No pedals.**

Keep the spec sheet and the purchase receipt with your records, ideally as a phone photo.

### 8.5 Restrictions most likely to produce a valid citation

These restrictions rest on clear text or a clearly stated City position, and the facts are easy to prove:
- riding on any **sidewalk in Dallas**;
- riding on the **Katy Trail**, White Rock Lake or any Dallas park;
- riding anywhere public in **Highland Park**;
- riding anywhere in **University Park** outside the SMU segments, or while under 18;
- riding on any road posted **above 35 mph** other than an intersection crossing;
- riding at **night without compliant lights**;
- **DWI or public intoxication**.

## Conclusion

Litigation will not widen the lawful map much, but careful reading will. Every validity attack is either untested or weak. The places where a private rider gains real ground are **applicability** questions:
- the Dallas curfew and slow zones appear to bind only rentals;
- University Park's 10 mph rule is phrased as a duty on rental operators;
- the Dallas "35 or higher" threshold may live only in rental-program language.

A single reading of the current Dallas § 28-41.1.1 text, together with the adoption records of Highland Park Ord. 2042 and University Park Ord. 18-048/18-049, would settle most of the "uncertain" cells in this report. That document work is worth more than any preemption brief.

The second lesson is that the Park Cities are effectively islands closed to riding inside an open Dallas grid. Highland Park allows no public riding at all. University Park's lawful zone is a few blocks around SMU, plus a campus whose rules are set by a private owner. That makes DART rail and Dallas's 30-mph streets the realistic network. Because the binding variable is often a posted sign or a city-limit line rather than a statute, the most protective habit is a documented route that has been checked at the curb. If you are cited, the case will usually turn on whether the State can prove where you were and what you were riding.
