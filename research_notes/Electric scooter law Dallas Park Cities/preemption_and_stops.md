## A1. Municipal power, and whether Dallas, Highland Park and University Park are home-rule

### Takeaway
All three are home-rule municipalities. Highland Park's home-rule charter dates from 1975 and was revised in 2004. University Park adopted its home-rule charter in 1989. Dallas is a long-standing home-rule city (see Gaps). A home-rule city does not need the Legislature to grant it power over scooters. It looks to state law only for *limits*, and a court will find a limit only where the Legislature stated it with "unmistakable clarity." The real question is therefore whether Transportation Code §542.201 and §551.352 limit these cities. Whether they have the power in the first place is not in doubt. **(CE)**

### Inferences
- Because all three are home-rule cities, a challenger must find a state statute that limits them with unmistakable clarity. In practice the candidates are Transp. ### Inferences
1. **A sidewalk or street ban adopted with a recited safety determination is expressly authorized by §551.352(b).** It therefore does not "conflict" under §542.201. Vulnerability is **low**. **(CE on the text. No case was found, but none is needed.)**
2. **Must the safety determination be a formal finding?** The statute requires that the governing body "determines" the prohibition is "necessary in the interest of safety." It prescribes no form, such as written findings, a hearing, or evidence.
   - *For the city:* ordinances are presumed valid (*Brookside Village*). A preamble or recital, or a council briefing such as Dallas's 2018 deck, likely suffices. Courts defer to legislative safety judgments, and a challenger must show the council made no determination at all or acted arbitrarily.
   - *For the challenger:* §542.201 permits a conflicting ordinance only where "expressly authorized," and here the authorization has a condition precedent. Without any safety determination in the ordinance or record, the ordinance arguably falls outside (b) and conflicts with (d)'s sidewalk permission.
   - **(AMB.) Likely outcome:** a court would infer the determination from enactment unless the record affirmatively shows a non-safety motive. A challenge has a textual hook but faces a strong presumption of validity.
3. **Parks and trails.** Subsection (b) does not mention parks, trails or paths. Two readings follow, and both point the same way for most trails.
   - *Shared-use park trails* (pedestrians plus bicycles) are not a "path set aside for the **exclusive** operation of bicycles," and are probably not a "sidewalk" (which is part of a *street*). Subsection (d) then grants no state-law right there, and a ban rests on home-rule and park powers. §542.202(a)(6) (speed of vehicles in a public park) shows the Legislature contemplated local park traffic rules. **Vulnerability: low (SI).**
   - *Bans on exclusive bicycle paths* are the **strongest preemption argument** available to a challenger. Subsection (d) affirmatively allows riding there, (b) lists only "street, highway, or sidewalk," and §542.201 bars conflicting rules "unless expressly authorized." The *expressio unius* argument is that the omission of paths was deliberate. Counter-arguments:
     - §542.202(a)(4) lets local authorities regulate bicycle operation, and (d)'s second sentence applies bicycle provisions to scooters.
     - §551.106 lets local authorities set speed limits on bike paths.
     - "May operate" can be read as a default rule of the road rather than an unmistakably clear limit on cities.
     - *Dallas Merchant's* harmonization canon (see A3).
     
     **(SI. Moderate vulnerability; untested.)**
   - A further wrinkle: a public bike trail might itself be a "highway" if bicycles count as "vehicles" and the trail is "open to the public for vehicular travel." That would bring it within (b). **(SI)**
4. **A citywide blanket ban.** Subsection (b) says "a street, highway, or sidewalk." Under Gov't Code §311.012(b) the singular includes the plural, so a ban covering all streets and sidewalks is textually authorized if a safety determination is made. The challenger's argument is that (b) contemplates location-specific safety judgments, and that is weak. **(SI; low–moderate vulnerability.)**
5. **A rule that permits what state law forbids conflicts.** For example, allowing scooters on roads posted above 35 mph would contradict §551.352(a). **(CE)**
6. **Age, helmet, curfew, parking, and dockless-operator permit rules** are not addressed by §551.352. They are likely "additional" non-conflicting regulations, or exercises of street control under §311.001. **(SI; low vulnerability.)** One untested argument: a fine above the subtitle's $1–$200 range (§542.401) for conduct the state already regulates could be called a conflict. Ordinance fines up to $500 under LGC §54.001 are usually defended as independent ordinance offenses. **(SI)**
7. **Bicycle-rule importation.** Because (d) applies bicycle provisions to scooters, a city could also rely on its §551.106 power to "prohibit bicycles on sidewalks." The specific rule in §551.352(b), with its safety condition, likely controls over that general power ("except as otherwise provided by this section"). **(SI)**

## A3. Controlling Texas Supreme Court preemption doctrine, and traffic-specific preemption

### Takeaway
Home-rule ordinances survive unless the Legislature limited them with "unmistakable clarity." Courts also harmonize a statute and an ordinance where any reasonable construction leaves both in effect (*Dallas Merchant's*). Once a statute *expressly* restricts local regulation, however, the Court enforces its text strictly (*Dallas Merchant's*, *Laredo*, *BCCA*). Transp. Code §542.201 is exactly that kind of express clause for the Rules of the Road. Scooter cases will therefore turn on whether a particular rule "conflicts" with §551.352, not on whether the Legislature intended to preempt. **(CE as doctrine; SI as applied.)**

### Cited Findings
- ***Dallas Merchant's & Concessionaire's Ass'n v. City of Dallas*, 852 S.W.2d 489 (Tex. 1993).** The Alcoholic Beverage Code preempted Dallas's ordinance barring alcohol sales within 300 feet of a residential area **[V]**. Rules it states:
  - If the Legislature decides to preempt a subject normally within a home-rule city's broad powers, it must do so with "unmistakable clarity" **[V, via secondary quotation]**.
  - "A general law and a city ordinance will not be held repugnant to each other if any other reasonable construction leaving both in effect can be reached" **[V-quote as quoted by *City of Santa Fe v. Young* (Tex. App. 1997)]**.
  - "[T]he mere fact that the legislature has enacted a law addressing a subject does not mean the subject matter is completely preempted" **[T-quote]**.
  - The preemption rested on the ABC's express anti-local-regulation clause (ABC §109.57) **[T]**.
  
  Sources: [Denton Legistar copy of opinion](https://denton-tx.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=D1B48C8C-4AF6-441B-840D-75214310762F&ID=3273754&M=F); [City of Santa Fe v. Young (FindLaw)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-court-of-appeals/1013171.html)
- ***City of Laredo v. Laredo Merchants Ass'n*, 550 S.W.3d 586 (Tex. 2018) (No. 16-0748, decided June 22, 2018, unanimous)** **[V]** — [Justia opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2018/16-0748.html); [FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/tx-supreme-court/1939485.html); [National Law Review summary](https://natlawreview.com/article/texas-supreme-court-strikes-down-local-bag-ban)
  - Laredo's checkout-bag ban was held preempted by Health & Safety Code §361.0961. That section bars a local government from prohibiting or restricting, "for solid waste management purposes, the sale or use of a container or package in a manner not authorized by state law."
  - The Court found the ordinance served solid-waste purposes and that bags are "containers." The Fourth Court of Appeals had reversed the trial court, which had upheld the ordinance. Justice Guzman's concurrence urged the Legislature to act.
  - The City's argument that the Act did not "clearly and unmistakably" preempt bag bans failed. The search could not verify the Court's own exact phrasing on the standard **[V]**.

## A6. Procedural vehicle: challenging an ordinance's validity in municipal court

### Takeaway
In Texas, a penal ordinance's validity is ordinarily litigated **as a defense in the criminal (municipal court) prosecution**. A civil suit is generally available only where enforcement threatens irreparable injury to vested property rights. A defendant should raise preemption or invalidity **in writing before trial**, as a motion to quash or exception to the complaint, because an ordinance void for conflict with state law means no offense was charged. The defendant must then preserve the point through trial and appeal. **(CE in general shape; [T] for the specific authorities.)**

### Cited Findings
- ***State v. Morales*, 869 S.W.2d 941 (Tex. 1994)** **[T]** — [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22869+S.W.2d+941%22+State+v.+Morales). Civil courts sitting in equity generally lack jurisdiction to declare a penal statute or ordinance invalid, or to enjoin its enforcement, unless the law is unconstitutional *and* its enforcement threatens irreparable injury to vested property rights. Validity is ordinarily for courts exercising criminal jurisdiction.
- **Civil route, for operators with property interests [T]:**
  - Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §37.006(b): in any proceeding involving the validity of a municipal ordinance, the municipality must be made a party. If constitutionality is challenged, the Attorney General must be served — [official CP ch. 37](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CP/htm/CP.37.htm)
  - *City of El Paso v. Heinrich*, 284 S.W.3d 366 (Tex. 2009), recognizes that the UDJA waives immunity for suits challenging the validity of ordinances — [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22284+S.W.3d+366%22+Heinrich)
- **Criminal route [T]:**
  - An exception to the substance of the charging instrument that "it does not appear therefrom that an offense against the law was committed" (Code Crim. Proc. art. 27.08(1)) — [official CR ch. 27](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.27.htm)
  - Defects of form or substance are waived if not raised before trial on the merits. For justice and municipal courts this was formerly art. 45.019(f). **Chapter 45 was recodified into Chapter 45A effective January 1, 2025 by HB 4504 (88th Leg.).** Verify current article numbers.
- **Facial challenges [T]:**
  - *Karenev v. State*, 281 S.W.3d 428 (Tex. Crim. App. 2009): a facial constitutional challenge to a penal statute is forfeited if not raised in the trial court — [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22281+S.W.3d+428%22+Karenev)
  - *Ex parte Weise*, 55 S.W.3d 617 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001): pretrial habeas is available for facial challenges.
  - *Ex parte Ingram*, 533 S.W.3d 887 (Tex. Crim. App. 2017): as-applied challenges generally are not cognizable on pretrial habeas.
- **Proof of the ordinance [T]:** Tex. R. Evid. 204 allows judicial notice of Texas municipal ordinances.
- **Appeals [T]:** from a non-record municipal court, by trial de novo in county court; from a municipal court of record, on the record under Gov't Code ch. 30.
- **Publication and adoption [T, low confidence on details]:** LGC §52.011 (Type A general-law) and §52.013 (home-rule) govern publication of ordinances that impose penalties. LGC §51.003 creates a conclusive presumption of validity for municipal acts not challenged within three years, but excepts acts void when they occurred — [official LGC ch. 52](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/LG/htm/LG.52.htm); [official LGC ch. 51](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/LG/htm/LG.51.htm)

### Inferences
- **What to raise.** A rider cited under a Dallas, Highland Park or University Park ordinance can argue, before trial:
  1. preemption, i.e. conflict with §551.352(d) without (b) authorization (A2 #2 and #3);
  2. that the ordinance was not properly adopted or published under the charter and LGC ch. 52; and
  3. that the ordinance does not reach the location or conduct charged (for example, a "sidewalk" ban applied to a park trail).
  
  These are legal questions for the judge. **(CE that they may be raised; SI on their merits.)**
- **Facial versus as-applied.** A facial preemption claim attacks every application. An as-applied claim argues, for example, that the place was an exclusive bike path outside (b). The as-applied version is generally litigated at trial, not on pretrial habeas. **(CE as to procedure, [T].)**
- **The ordinance-adoption record is discoverable public information.** Agenda, minutes, preamble findings and briefing (Public Information Act) are how a defendant tests whether a §551.352(b) safety determination was made. **(SI)**

## A7. Overall honest likelihood assessment (synthesis for Part A)

### Takeaway
A properly adopted Dallas, Highland Park or University Park ban on scooters on **streets or sidewalks**, with any safety rationale in the record, is **unlikely to be invalidated**. §551.352(b) expressly authorizes it, and §542.201 does not apply to authorized rules. The realistic openings are narrower:
1. bans on **exclusive bicycle paths** (moderate, untested);
2. sidewalk bans with **no safety determination at all** (low–moderate);

## B1. Basis for the stop: reasonable suspicion, *Heien*, *DeFillippo*, art. 38.23, and suspicion versus proof at trial

### Takeaway
An officer may briefly detain a scooter rider on reasonable suspicion of any offense, including a Class C. A reasonable mistake of law can supply that suspicion (*Heien*). An officer may also rely on a duly enacted ordinance even if a court later voids it (*DeFillippo*). Conviction is a separate matter: it requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the conduct actually violated a valid law. A lawful stop does not establish guilt, and an invalid ordinance defeats the charge even if the stop was lawful. Suppression under art. 38.23 rarely matters in scooter cases, because the officer's pre-stop observation is not a fruit of the stop. **(CE for the federal rules, [T]; Texas Court of Criminal Appeals treatment of *Heien* is a gap.)**

### Cited Findings
- ***Heien v. North Carolina*, 574 U.S. 54 (2014) (8–1)** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/574/54/)
  - Reasonable suspicion can rest on a reasonable mistake of law. The officer stopped a car with one working brake light, reasonably but mistakenly reading the statute to require two **[T]**.
  - "To be reasonable is not to be perfect, and so the Fourth Amendment allows for some mistakes on the part of government officials, giving them 'fair leeway for enforcing the law in the community's protection.'" **[T-quote]**
  - "The Fourth Amendment tolerates only reasonable mistakes, and those mistakes—whether of fact or of law—must be objectively reasonable. We do not examine the subjective understanding of the particular officer involved." **[T-quote]**
  - "[A]n officer can gain no Fourth Amendment advantage through a sloppy study of the laws he is duty-bound to enforce." **[T-quote]**
  - The majority accepted that the government cannot *impose criminal liability* based on a mistaken understanding of the law, and distinguished that from justifying a stop **[T, paraphrase]**.
  - Justice Kagan's concurrence (joined by Ginsburg): a mistake of law is reasonable only when the statute is genuinely ambiguous, "so doubtful in construction" that a reasonable judge could agree with the officer, so that overturning the officer's reading "requires hard interpretive work" **[T-quote]**.
- ***Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979):** "Police are charged to enforce laws until and unless they are declared unconstitutional. The enactment of a law forecloses speculation by enforcement officers concerning its constitutionality—with the possible exception of a law so grossly and flagrantly unconstitutional that any person of reasonable prudence would be bound to see its flaws." **[T-quote]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/443/31/)
- ***Whren v. United States*, 517 U.S. 806 (1996):** a stop supported by probable cause of a traffic violation is valid regardless of the officer's subjective motive **[T]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/517/806/)
- ***Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015):** a stop may last no longer than its "mission" (addressing the violation, plus ordinary inquiries such as checking ID and warrants) without independent reasonable suspicion **[T]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/575/348/)
- **Texas reasonable-suspicion standard [T]:** *Derichsweiler v. State*, 348 S.W.3d 906 (Tex. Crim. App. 2011). The officer needs specific, articulable facts that, with rational inferences, would lead him reasonably to conclude the person "is, has been, or soon will be engaged in criminal activity." The standard is objective and based on the totality of the circumstances. On a motion to suppress a warrantless detention, the State bears the burden once the defendant shows the detention was warrantless (*Amador v. State*, 221 S.W.3d 666 (Tex. Crim. App. 2007); *Ford v. State*, 158 S.W.3d 488 (Tex. Crim. App. 2005)). Source: [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22348+S.W.3d+906%22+Derichsweiler)
- **Code Crim. Proc. art. 38.23 [T-quote]** — [official CR ch. 38](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.38.htm)
  - (a): "No evidence obtained by an officer or other person in violation of any provisions of the Constitution or laws of the State of Texas, or of the Constitution or laws of the United States of America, shall be admitted in evidence against the accused on the trial of any criminal case." Where the evidence raises a disputed fact issue, the jury is instructed to disregard evidence it believes, or reasonably doubts, was illegally obtained.
  - (b): the only statutory good-faith exception is objective good-faith reliance on a warrant issued by a neutral magistrate on probable cause.
- **Burden at trial [T]:** Penal Code §2.01: "no person may be convicted of an offense unless each element of the offense is proved beyond a reasonable doubt" — [official PE ch. 2](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.2.htm)
- **Common lawful stop bases for scooter riders.** Because §551.352(d) applies bicycle rules to scooters ([V] Richardson PD), bicycle rules apply. Examples include riding at night without a white front lamp visible at 500 feet and a rear red reflector or lamp (§551.104(b)) **[T]**, and failing to ride as near as practicable to the right curb (§551.103) **[T]**. State law also limits scooters to streets posted at 35 mph or less (§551.352(a)) **[V]**. Sources: [official TN ch. 551 PDF](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/TN/pdf/TN.551.pdf); [Richardson PD](https://www.richardsonpolice.net/i-want-to/apply-for/motorized-scooters)

### Inferences
- **Where an ordinance exists** (e.g., a Dallas, Highland Park or University Park sidewalk ban), a stop for violating it is lawful under *DeFillippo* even if the ordinance is later held preempted. Preemption is not a "gross and flagrant" defect. The invalidity still defeats the *ordinance charge* itself. **(CE federally, [T]. Whether Texas art. 38.23 adopts *DeFillippo* is not verified.)**
- **Where no ordinance exists** and an officer stops a rider for "riding on the sidewalk," the mistake is likely unreasonable under *Heien*, because §551.352(d) clearly permits sidewalk riding. The stop would be unlawful, but suppression helps only for evidence discovered *after* the stop (intoxication, contraband, a false-ID charge), not for the observed riding. **(SI)**
- **Reasonable suspicion is far below proof beyond a reasonable doubt.** At trial the State must prove that the device is a "motor-assisted scooter" (or whatever the ordinance covers), the location (street, sidewalk, or trail type), the governing ordinance, and its validity. **(CE)**

### Gaps
- **Texas Court of Criminal Appeals decisions adopting, applying or limiting *Heien* under the Fourth Amendment, Tex. Const. art. I §9, and art. 38.23 were not verified this session.** Pre-*Heien*, Texas intermediate courts generally held that a mistake of law could not support a stop. Do not cite specific Texas mistake-of-law cases from these notes without independent verification.

## B2. Arrest versus citation for fine-only offenses (CCP arts. 14.01, 14.06; Transp. Code ch. 543; *Atwater*)

### Takeaway
An officer **may** make a custodial arrest for a fine-only offense committed in the officer's presence, and that is constitutional (*Atwater*; art. 14.01(b); §543.001). Citation instead of arrest is the norm and is discretionary (art. 14.06(b)). §543.004 makes a written notice to appear **mandatory only for speeding and open-container** offenses. Chapter 543, Subchapter A covers violations "of this subtitle" (Title 7, Subtitle C, which includes ch. 551), so it applies to state-law scooter violations even though scooters are not motor vehicles. Pure **city-ordinance** violations fall under CCP arts. 14.01 and 14.06. **(CE for the rules, [T]; SI on how Ch. 543 maps onto ordinance charges.)**

### Cited Findings
- ***Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001)** (a Texas seat-belt case): "If an officer has probable cause to believe that an individual has committed even a very minor criminal offense in his presence, he may, without violating the Fourth Amendment, arrest the offender." **[T-quote]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/532/318/)
- **CCP art. 14.01(b):** "A peace officer may arrest an offender without a warrant for any offense committed in his presence or within his view." **[T-quote]** — [official CR ch. 14](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.14.htm)
- **CCP art. 14.06(b) [T, paraphrase]:**
  - A peace officer charging a person with a **Class C misdemeanor**, other than **public intoxication (Penal Code §49.02)** or an Alcoholic Beverage Code ch. 106 (minor) offense, *may*, instead of taking the person before a magistrate, issue a citation.
  - The citation contains written notice of when and where to appear, the person's name and address, the offense charged, and required admonitions.
  - Art. 14.06(c) extends a similar citation option to listed Class A/B offenses (e.g., small-quantity marijuana possession) in counties with a cite-and-release policy.
- **Transp. Code ch. 543 [T]** — [official TN ch. 543](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/TN/htm/TN.543.htm)
  - **§543.001:** "Any peace officer may arrest without warrant a person found committing a violation of this subtitle." **[T-quote]**
  - **§543.002:** the person must be taken immediately before a magistrate if, among other things, the person "demands an immediate appearance before a magistrate or refuses to make a written promise to appear in court."
  - **§543.003:** an officer who does not take the person before a magistrate prepares a written notice to appear.
  - **§543.004(a)** makes the notice mandatory for speeding and open-container (§49.031) offenses when the person signs a promise to appear. **(c):** "The offenses specified by Subsection (a) are the only offenses for which issuance of a written notice to appear is mandatory." **[T-quote]**
  - **§543.005:** to secure release, the person signs a written promise to appear.
- **Scope [T plus V].** "This subtitle" in ch. 543 refers to Title 7, Subtitle C (Rules of the Road), which includes ch. 551. Ch. 543's arrest and notice provisions concern persons and offenses, not motor vehicles, so §551.352(e)'s motor-vehicle exclusion ([V-quote](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/pdf/SB00549S.pdf)) does not appear to displace them.

### Inferences
- **For a state-law scooter violation** (e.g., riding on a road posted above 35 mph, or without a night lamp): §543.001 permits arrest, but the usual practice is a written notice to appear signed as a promise to appear. Refusing to sign is a ground for being taken before a magistrate. **(CE as to text, [T].)**
- **For a city-ordinance scooter violation:** this is a Class C offense under Penal Code §12.41(3), so arrest under art. 14.01(b) is available and a citation is optional under art. 14.06(b). Custodial arrest is lawful but unusual for compliant riders. **(SI as to Ch. 543's application, CE as to art. 14.)**
- **If the rider appears intoxicated,** public intoxication (§49.02) is excluded from art. 14.06(b) citation, so a custodial arrest is typical. **(CE, [T].)**

### Gaps

- The current text of art. 14.06 after any 2025 amendments was not verified.

## B3. Identification: Penal Code §38.02 and license display under Transp. Code §521.025

### Takeaway
A scooter rider is **not required to hold, carry or show a driver's license**. §551.352(e) removes Title 7 motor-vehicle provisions from scooters, and ch. 521 applies to "operat[ing] a motor vehicle." Under Penal Code §38.02, a person who is merely **detained** commits no offense by declining to give name, address or date of birth. Giving **false** information while detained **is** an offense. After a **lawful arrest**, refusing to give those three items is an offense. In practice, refusing to identify during a Class C stop usually leads to a custodial arrest, because a citation in lieu of arrest needs an identified person to sign a promise to appear. After the arrest, §38.02(a) applies. **(CE for the statutes, [T]; the practical sequence is an inference.)**

### Cited Findings
- **Penal Code §38.02 [T-quote]** — [official PE ch. 38](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.38.htm)
  - (a): "A person commits an offense if he intentionally refuses to give his name, residence address, or date of birth to a peace officer who has lawfully arrested the person and requested the information."
  - (b): "A person commits an offense if he intentionally gives a false or fictitious name, residence address, or date of birth to a peace officer who has: (1) lawfully arrested the person; (2) lawfully detained the person; or (3) requested the information from a person that the peace officer has good cause to believe is a witness to a criminal offense."
  - Grading: (a) is Class C; (b) is Class B (enhanced if the person was a fugitive).
- ***Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial Dist. Court*, 542 U.S. 177 (2004):** a State *may* require a suspect to disclose a name during a valid *Terry* stop. Texas's §38.02(a) instead requires it only after lawful arrest **[T]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/542/177/)
- **Transp. Code §521.021 and §521.025 [T]** — [official TN ch. 521](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/TN/htm/TN.521.htm)
  - §521.021: a person may not "operate a motor vehicle on a highway in this state unless the person holds a driver's license."
  - §521.025(a): a person required to hold a license must possess it "while operating a motor vehicle" and display it on demand of a magistrate, court officer, or peace officer.
- **§551.352(e):** "A provision of this title applicable to a motor vehicle does not apply to a motor-assisted scooter." **[V-quote]** — [SB 549 text showing existing law](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/pdf/SB00549S.pdf)
- The failed 2019 SB 549 would have *added* a 16-and-licensed requirement, which confirms none existed **[V]** — [Plural Policy SB 549](https://open.pluralpolicy.com/tx/bills/86/SB549)
- **Minors [T, moderate confidence; verify]:** Penal Code §8.07 provides that children under 10 cannot be prosecuted, and that children aged 10–14 are presumed incapable of committing fine-only offenses unless the State rebuts the presumption by a preponderance — [official PE ch. 8](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.8.htm). Local ordinances may instead target the custodian (Katy example) **[V]** — [Katy Art. 12.06](https://ecode360.com/38861632)

### Inferences
- No license or ID is required to ride a device that meets the §551.351 definition. Declining to give a name during a mere detention is lawful but practically risky. Lying is a crime. **(CE, [T])**
- If a device falls **outside** §551.351, for example a seated machine with no deck, or one that cannot be human-propelled, it may be classed as a moped or motorcycle. Licensing, registration and §521.025 could then apply. **(SI)**

### Gaps
- No Texas case applying §38.02 specifically to scooter or bicycle riders was verified.
- The 2019–2025 amendment history of §8.07 was not verified.

## B4. Impoundment or seizure of a privately owned scooter

### Takeaway
No Texas statute specifically authorizes police to impound a *privately owned* motor-assisted scooter for a Class C violation. Abandoned-vehicle law (Transp. Code ch. 683) is built around motor vehicles, which §551.352(e) excludes. Dockless-vehicle ordinances address operators' rental fleets. Seizure is therefore governed by the Fourth Amendment. Police may hold the scooter for safekeeping or inventory **when the rider is arrested** and no reasonable alternative exists, under a standardized policy. Seizing it "as evidence" of a fine-only offense is hard to justify, and Class C offenses do not trigger Chapter 59 forfeiture. **(SI overall; [T].)**

### Cited Findings
- ***South Dakota v. Opperman*, 428 U.S. 364 (1976)** and ***Colorado v. Bertine*, 479 U.S. 367 (1987):** inventory and impoundment are permitted under standardized criteria as community caretaking **[T]** — [Justia Opperman](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/428/364/); [Justia Bertine](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/479/367/)
- **Texas:** impoundment must be reasonable. Texas courts look at whether alternatives existed, such as releasing the property to a companion or leaving it lawfully secured. One example is *Benavides v. State*, 600 S.W.2d 809 (Tex. Crim. App. 1980) **[T, moderate confidence]** — [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22600+S.W.2d+809%22+Benavides)
- ***Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990):** plain-view seizure requires that the item's incriminating character be "immediately apparent," i.e. probable cause that it is evidence or contraband **[T]** — [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/496/128/)
- **CCP ch. 59 forfeiture** reaches "contraband" as defined in art. 59.01, mainly property connected to felonies and listed offenses, not Class C traffic or ordinance violations **[T]** — [official CR ch. 59](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CR/htm/CR.59.htm)
- **Transp. Code ch. 683 (abandoned motor vehicles)** applies by its terms to motor vehicles. §551.352(e) excludes Title 7 motor-vehicle provisions from scooters **[V-quote for (e)]; [T for ch. 683]** — [official TN ch. 683](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/TN/htm/TN.683.htm)

### Inferences
- For a compliant rider who receives a citation, there is no evident lawful basis to keep the scooter. The rider walks it away, or it is released to a companion. **(SI)**
- When the rider is arrested, a policy-based impound or inventory is likely lawful, especially if no one can take custody. **(SI; depends on department policy.)**
- City dockless ordinances that authorize removal or impoundment of improperly parked *rental* devices should not be read to reach private scooters unless their text says so. Check each city's text. **(SI)**

### Gaps
- The text of the Dallas, Highland Park and University Park impoundment provisions, and police policies, were not reviewed (outside scope or unavailable).
- No Texas case on seizure of a private scooter or bicycle for a fine-only offense was found.

## B5. Recording police

### Takeaway
Recording police performing duties in public is protected by the First Amendment in the Fifth Circuit, which covers Texas (*Turner v. Driver*, 2017), subject to reasonable time, place and manner limits. Texas is a one-party-consent state for recording conversations. Penal Code §38.15 (interference) has a "speech only" defense, but physically impeding an officer is not protected. As of the researcher's knowledge, Texas has **not enacted** a statutory "buffer zone" (such as 25 feet) for approaching officers. A 2023 bill to create one did not pass. The 2025 session is unverified. **(CE for *Turner* and the statutes, [T]; buffer-law status AMB.)**

### Cited Findings
- ***Turner v. Driver*, 848 F.3d 678 (5th Cir. 2017):** recognizes a First Amendment right to film police carrying out their duties in public, subject to reasonable time, place and manner restrictions. Qualified immunity was granted because the right was not clearly established before 2017 **[T]** — [Google Scholar search](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Turner+v.+Driver%22+848+F.3d+678)
- **Penal Code §38.15** (interference with public duties): it is an offense to, with criminal negligence, interrupt, disrupt, impede or otherwise interfere with a peace officer performing a duty. Subsection (d): "It is a defense to prosecution under this section that the interruption, disruption, impediment, or interference alleged consisted of speech only." **[T-quote]** — [official PE ch. 38](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.38.htm)
- **Penal Code §16.02:** interception is lawful where the person is a party to the communication or one party consents **[T]** — [official PE ch. 16](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.16.htm)
- **Buffer-zone bills [T, moderate confidence]:** a 2023 Texas bill (recalled as HB 2918, 88th Leg.) would have made it an offense to approach within 25 feet of an officer after a warning. It did not become law.

### Inferences
- A rider or bystander may record a scooter stop from a non-interfering distance and may keep recording while complying with lawful orders. **(CE)**
- An officer's order to step back a reasonable distance for safety is likely enforceable under §38.15 as conduct, not speech. **(SI)**

### Gaps
- **Whether the 89th Legislature (2025) enacted any buffer-zone or recording restriction was not verified.** The search budget was exhausted. Treat this as open until checked.
- The bill number and history of the 2023 buffer bill are unverified.

## B6. Texas appellate cases on scooter stops, DWI on a scooter, and scooter classification

### Takeaway
No Texas appellate decision on a motor-assisted-scooter stop, a scooter DWI, or scooter classification was verified this session. Analytically, §551.352(e) excludes only *Title 7 (Transportation Code)* motor-vehicle provisions. The Penal Code's DWI chapter uses its own cross-referenced definition of "motor vehicle," so whether an e-scooter is a "motor vehicle" for **DWI** is **subject to interpretation**. Public intoxication (§49.02) applies regardless. **(SI)**

### Cited Findings
- **The statutory definition** of "motor-assisted scooter" includes a "gas or electric motor not exceeding 40 cubic centimeters," a deck, and the "ability to be propelled by human power alone" **[V]** — [FindLaw §551.351](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/transportation-code/transp-sect-551-351/)
- **§551.352(e)** excludes Title 7 motor-vehicle provisions from scooters **[V-quote]** — [SB 549 text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/86R/billtext/pdf/SB00549S.pdf)
- **Penal Code §49.01** defines "motor vehicle" for ch. 49 by cross-reference to §32.34(a). §49.04 (DWI) requires "operating a motor vehicle in a public place" while intoxicated **[T]** — [official PE ch. 49](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.49.htm). The current target text of the §32.34(a) definition is **not verified**. Historically it was a broad "device in, on, or by which a person or property is or may be transported or drawn on a highway" formulation; it may now point to Transp. Code §501.002.
- **Penal Code §49.02** (public intoxication): appearing in a public place while intoxicated to the degree that the person may endanger the person or another is a Class C offense, and it is excluded from art. 14.06(b) citation **[T]** — [official PE ch. 49](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.49.htm)

### Inferences
- If ch. 49's definition is the broad "device … transported … on a highway" formulation, an e-scooter ridden on a public street probably qualifies, and the Title 7 exclusion in §551.352(e) would not control the Penal Code. If the definition now tracks Transp. Code §501.002 (registration-based), the answer is less clear. **(SI)**
- Classification disputes are a real defense lever for **ordinance** charges. Devices that exceed the §551.351 parameters (no deck, cannot be human-propelled) may not be "motor-assisted scooters," and an ordinance limited to that term may not reach them. They may, however, fall into moped or motorcycle categories that carry heavier requirements. **(SI)**

### Gaps
- No Texas appellate case on scooter DWI, scooter stops, or §551.351 classification was found or verified.
- The current text of Penal Code §32.34(a) and §49.01(3) was not verified.

Code chs. 541–543, 551; LGC chs. 51–54; Penal Code §§38.02, 38.15, 49.01; CCP arts. 14.06 and 38.23; and (2) a case-law search (CourtListener or Google Scholar) for "motor-assisted scooter," "551.352," and Texas CCA cases citing *Heien*.
